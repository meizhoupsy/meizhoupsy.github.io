Research

Understand how we learn and remember

We learn a great deal from the world, yet our memory to hold information is limited. How does the brain deal with this challenge? One possibility is that learning shapes how we prioritize what we need to remember. Through statistical learning, we can extract regularities across experiences, allowing us to organize new information around shared patterns. But this relation works in both directions: if learning changes what we hold in mind, how does what we hold in mind shape what we learn next?

My research investigates this interplay between statistical learning and working memory as learning unfolds. Combining behavioural, neural, and computational approaches, I examine how these interactions vary across individuals and contexts, studying neurotypical and neurodiverse populations such as developmental dyslexia.

Statistical Learning

How do we make sense of the world with regularities?

From infancy, we begin to discover regularities in the world around us: which features occur together, what happens more often, and which events predict what comes next. This capacity, known as statistical learning, helps us find structure in complex input and supports learning across domains such as language acquisition. Although it appears to be an effortless ability, it may involve multiple interacting mechanisms. When many features and patterns overlap, how do we determine which relations to learn? How does specific experience give rise to abstract knowledge that we can use, even when we cannot explicitly describe it? And how do we update our knowledge representations when the environment changes and predictions become less reliable?

My research addresses these questions by learners' behavioural responses and brain activities in complex inputs including different levels of uncertainty, across multiple sources of regularity, and higher-order abstract relations. I have been combining intrincate behavioral designs and neural measures. And building on this work, now I am developing computational approaches to test mechanistic explanations of how learning unfolds and how abstract knowledge emerges from individual experiences.

Memory Prioritization

What determines which information receives priority in memory?

We encounter more information than we can actively hold in mind. Working memory must therefore be selective: some information gains priority, while other information becomes less accessible. But what determines this selection? Our immediate goals matter, yet prioritization need not depend on deliberate choice. What we have learned about the environment may also influence which information receives priority, shaping memory even without an explicit intention to remember it.

My research examines how statistical learning shapes this selection process. I am particularly interested in the balance between abstract structure and item-specific detail: when does memory prioritize what experiences have in common, and when does it preserve what makes an individual event distinctive? I investigate how this balance changes with input predictability and encoding goals, and how learning and memory jointly shape the representations available to guide behavior.

Dyslexia

How do learning and memory mechanisms vary across individuals?

My research is grounded in fundamental questions about learning and memory, but I am also dedicated to understand how these processes vary across neurodiverse populations, with a current focus on developmental dyslexia. Dyslexia is characterized by their reading and spelling difficulties. and I ask Whether these difficulties derive from their inefficiency of extracting linguistic regularities supported by statistical learning.

I use modeling approaches and item-level analyses to study how responses vary with the properties of individual inputs. This approach aims to understand when particular ways of processing information may support or constrain learning. More broadly, I suggest a fine-grained view to look at dyslexia. Rather than simply labelling them with reading deficits, perceiving their learning characteristic may better capture their learning. And more extremely, it may reflect a different learning strategy, which may not be a "deficit". For example, one possibility I investigate is that dyslexia may use more exploration for unfamiliar information and less exploitation of familiar knowledge. This strategy may not always be detrivate in all learning contexts, but support their strengths in some aspects such as creativity.
