Research

How do we learn and remember in a complex and uncertain world?

We continuously absorb information from and interact with the world, yet our memory to hold information is limited. How does the brain cope with this fundamental challenge? One important mechanism is that learning helps us determine what matters and what should be remembered. Through statistical learning, we can extract regularities across experiences, allowing us to organize new information around shared patterns. Crucially, this relation is bidirection: while learning shapes what we hold in mind, the contents we hold in working memory further influence what we attend to and learn next.

My research investigates this dynamic interplay between statistical learning and working memory as learning unfolds. By combining behavioural, neural, and computational approaches, I examine how these processes operate under environmental uncertainties and vary across individuals, including neurotypical and neurodiverse populations such as developmental dyslexia.

Statistical Learning

Learning regularities and building knowledge.

From infancy, we begin to discover regularities in the world around us: which features occur together, what happens more often, and which events predict what comes next. This capacity, known as statistical learning, helps us find structure in complex input and supports learning across domains such as language acquisition. Although it appears to be an effortless ability, the underlying mechanisms are complex. When many features and patterns overlap, how do we determine which relations to learn? How does specific experience give rise to abstract knowledge that we can use, even when we cannot explicitly articulate what we learn? And how do we update our knowledge representations when the environment changes and predictions become less reliable?

My research addresses these questions by investigating learners' behaviour and neural activity as they encounter complex input involving different levels of uncertainty, multiple sources of regularity, and higher-order abstract relations. Furthermore, building on this work, I am incorporating computational modelling to identify the computational principles that support learning and shape the development of memory representations.

Memory Prioritization

Which information receives priority in memory?

We encounter more information than we can actively hold in mind. Working memory must therefore be selective: some information gains priority, while other information becomes less accessible. But what determines this selection? Part of my research examines how statistical learning shapes this selection process. In other words, what we have learned about the environment may influence which information receives priority, shaping memory even without an explicit intention to remember it.

I am particularly interested in the balance between abstract structure and item-specific detail: when does memory prioritize the abstract structure shared across experiences, and when does it preserve what makes an individual event distinctive? How does this balance change with external environmental uncertainty and internal learning goals.

Dyslexia

Beyond deficit, how does learning differ in dyslexia?

My research is grounded in fundamental questions about learning and memory, but I am also dedicated to understand how these processes vary across neurodiverse populations. My current word focuses on dyslexia, which is characterized by persistent difficulties in reading and spelling, althought its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Statistical learning and working memory, two cognitive abilities that support language and reading acquisition, are especially relevant, as research suggests that both may function differently in individuals with dyslexia.

In my studies, I focus on examining item-level learning and modeling learning dynamics to capture more fine-grained characteristerics associated with dyslexia. For example, by investigating the relation between working memory and statistical learning at the item level, I found that children with dyslexia were more likely to explore unfamiliar inputs, whereas their typically developing peers relied on exploiting familiar and previously learned information. Such evidence moves beyond a purely deficit-based view of dyslexia and can inform more tailored intervention.
