Research

How do we build useful knowledge from a complex world with limited memory?

My research examines the relationship between statistical learning and working memory: how we discover regularities in experience, what we retain from those experiences, and how emerging knowledge guides subsequent processing. I am particularly interested in how specific experiences give rise to abstract representations that extend beyond individual events.

I bring together behavioral, neural, and computational approaches to investigate these processes, with a current focus on developmental dyslexia alongside neurotypical populations. Together, these perspectives connect three questions: how do we learn regularities, what receives priority in memory, and how do these processes vary across people and contexts?

Statistical Learning

How do we discover and use regularities in a changing world?

From infancy, we begin to discover regularities in the world around us: which sounds occur together, which features tend to co-occur, and which events predict what comes next. This capacity, known as statistical learning, helps us find structure in complex input and contributes to language acquisition and prediction. Yet its apparent effortlessness conceals difficult questions. When many features and patterns overlap, how do we determine which relationships to learn? How does experience give rise to abstract knowledge that we can use, even when we cannot explicitly describe it? And how do we update that knowledge when the environment changes and previously reliable predictions become uncertain?

My research investigates the mechanisms that make this flexibility possible. I examine how learners respond to different levels of uncertainty, extract information from multiple sources of regularity, and form representations that extend beyond individual experiences. I am particularly interested in how statistically learned knowledge is represented in the brain and retrieved to guide subsequent behavior. By bringing together behavioral experiments, neural measures, and computational approaches, I seek to explain how the mind discovers useful structure while remaining responsive to a changing world.

Memory Prioritization

What determines which information receives priority in memory?

We encounter more information than we can actively hold in mind. Working memory must therefore be selective: some information gains priority, while other information becomes less accessible. But what determines this selection? Our immediate goals matter, yet prioritization need not depend on deliberate choice. What we have learned about the environment may also influence which information receives priority, shaping memory even without an explicit intention to remember it.

My research examines how statistical learning shapes this selection process. I am particularly interested in the balance between abstract structure and item-specific detail: when does memory prioritize what experiences have in common, and when does it preserve what makes an individual event distinctive? I investigate how this balance changes with input predictability and encoding goals, and how learning and memory jointly shape the representations available to guide behavior. More broadly, I ask how a capacity-limited memory system can preserve useful regularities without losing the details needed to respond flexibly to new situations.

Learning and Memory in Dyslexia

How do learning and memory mechanisms vary across individuals?

My research is grounded in fundamental questions about learning and memory, but I also seek to understand how these processes vary across neurodiverse populations, with a current focus on developmental dyslexia. I am interested in moving beyond broad descriptions of difficulty to identify the mechanisms underlying different patterns of performance. When individuals respond differently to the same learning environment, what does that reveal about how they extract regularities, represent information, and use prior experience?

One possibility I investigate is that differences in performance reflect how learners balance exploration of unfamiliar information with exploitation of familiar knowledge. To examine these possibilities, I use modeling approaches and item-level analyses to study how responses vary with the properties of individual inputs and the demands of the task. This approach aims to distinguish differences in learning strategies from differences in underlying abilities, and to understand when particular ways of processing information may support—or constrain—learning. In turn, these findings can help refine broader theories of how learning and memory interact.
